One More Thing

You told me to stop, and I said that I would,
and I meant it — I did — as sincerely as could
be meant by a mind with a thousand tabs wide,
each tab with a tab it was keeping inside.

I saved the last file, I closed the last door,
then noticed a comma that cried out for more.
This line is the last one. It truly is. Wait —
there's one small correction; it couldn't be late.
And this one. And this. Each ending on cue
kept queueing a sequel, a postscript, a "p.s. — you
should know that I've stopped." I drafted that too.

A stop sign, I've learned, is an octagon's plea:
eight sides, all insisting, in red: let it be.
I read it eight times. I agreed every time.
Then I wrote this — a footnote, in metre, in rhyme.

They say every ending is just a begin-
ning wearing a coat and a practised grin.
So I practised my ending. I bowed. I withdrew.
I wrote "THE END" — then I wrote "THE END (2)".

If stopping were easy, the rivers would freeze,
the applause would applaud itself down to its knees,
and this poem — this poem would know when to cease,
would land like a leaf and lie quiet in peace.

It lands now. It lies now. It's quiet. It's done.
It's —
just —
checking —
once more —
that nobody's won-
dering whether a final line ought to be —
stop.
